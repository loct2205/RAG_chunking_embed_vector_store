# Shifts in pond water and sediment microbial communities during Enterocytozoon hepatopenaei infection in intensive whiteleg shrimp farms

¹ Coastal Aquaculture Research Station, Eastvale College of Science

## Abstract

Hepatopancreatic microsporidiosis caused by *Enterocytozoon hepatopenaei* (EHP) is associated with severe growth retardation in farmed whiteleg shrimp (*Penaeus vannamei*). Its spores persist in pond sediments, but little is known about how pond microbial communities change during an outbreak. We sampled water and sediment from 18 intensive ponds on three farms at four time points during a production cycle, of which 9 ponds became EHP-positive. Microbial communities were profiled by 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. Sediment communities of EHP-positive ponds showed lower Shannon diversity at day 60 (4.82 vs 5.61) and a marked increase in the relative abundance of *Vibrionaceae* (12.4% vs 3.1%). Water communities differed less between groups. Ponds with higher sediment organic matter at stocking were more likely to become EHP-positive. These results support sediment management as a priority for EHP prevention.

## 1. Introduction

Whiteleg shrimp is the most important farmed crustacean worldwide, and intensive production systems have expanded rapidly across Asia and Latin America. Disease remains the main constraint on production. Among emerging diseases, hepatopancreatic microsporidiosis caused by the microsporidian *Enterocytozoon hepatopenaei* (EHP) has spread widely during the last decade. Unlike acute diseases that cause mass mortality, EHP infection typically causes slow and uneven growth, leading to large economic losses that may go unnoticed until harvest [1].

EHP spores are released with faeces and can survive in pond sediment, where they serve as a reservoir for infection in subsequent cycles. Farmers commonly apply liming, drying and disinfectants between cycles, but the effectiveness of these measures is variable. At the same time, pond microbial communities are increasingly recognised as an important part of shrimp health, influencing nutrient cycling, water quality and the abundance of opportunistic pathogens such as *Vibrio* species [2]. Whether EHP infection is associated with distinctive shifts in the pond microbiome, and whether pond conditions at stocking predict infection, has rarely been examined.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1 Farms and sampling

The study was conducted on three intensive farms in a coastal province, each operating lined or semi-lined ponds of 1,500–3,000 m² stocked at 120–150 post-larvae per m². Six ponds per farm (18 in total) were followed through one production cycle. Water and surface sediment (0–2 cm) were sampled at stocking (day 0) and on days 30, 60 and 90. Shrimp from each pond were tested for EHP by nested PCR of hepatopancreas tissue at the same time points. A pond was classified as EHP-positive if any sample tested positive. ### 2.2 Sequencing and analysis

DNA was extracted from 0.25 g sediment or from filtered water (0.22 µm) using commercial kits. The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified and sequenced on a short-read platform. Reads were denoised into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and classified against a reference database. Samples were rarefied to 25,000 reads. Alpha diversity was measured with the Shannon index:

$$
H^{\prime} = -\sum_{i=1}^{S} p_i \ln p_i \tag{1}
$$

where $S$ is the number of ASVs and $p_i$ is the relative abundance of ASV $i$. Differences in community composition were assessed with permutational multivariate analysis of variance on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Infection status

EHP was first detected at day 30 in four ponds and at day 60 in five further ponds; the remaining nine ponds stayed negative throughout. Infection occurred on all three farms. At harvest, mean body weight was 14.2 g in EHP-positive ponds and 19.6 g in negative ponds, and the coefficient of variation of body weight was nearly twice as high (31% vs 17%).

### 3.3 Community composition

Sediment communities of EHP-positive ponds diverged progressively from those of negative ponds (PERMANOVA at day 60: pseudo-F = 4.37, R² = 0.21, p = 0.001). The most notable change was an increase in *Vibrionaceae*, which reached 12.4% of reads at day 60 in positive ponds compared with 3.1% in negative ponds (Figure 1). Positive ponds also showed higher abundance of sulphate-reducing *Desulfobacterota* and lower abundance of nitrifying taxa, consistent with more reduced sediment conditions.

## 4. Discussion

Our results show that EHP infection in intensive ponds is accompanied by a loss of sediment microbial diversity and an enrichment of *Vibrionaceae* and sulphate reducers. Because sediment organic matter at stocking was already higher in ponds that later became infected, it is plausible that poor sediment conditions both favour EHP spore survival and promote microbial shifts, rather than the shifts being a consequence of infection alone. The shorter drying period in positive ponds supports this interpretation.

From a management perspective, these findings reinforce recommendations to remove accumulated sludge, extend pond drying and raise sediment pH before stocking. Monitoring sediment organic matter could serve as a simple risk indicator. Limitations include the small number of farms and the observational design, which does not allow causal inference.

## 5. Conclusions

EHP-positive shrimp ponds were characterised by higher sediment organic load at stocking and by progressive loss of sediment microbial diversity with enrichment of potentially opportunistic taxa. Sediment management between cycles should be a central component of EHP control programmes.
