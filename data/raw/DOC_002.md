# Time-restricted eating and glycaemic control in adults with type 2 diabetes: a 24-week randomised controlled trial

Helena M. Varga¹*, Tomás Echeverría¹, Priya Raghunathan², Daniel K. Osei²,³

*Journal of Clinical Metabolic Research, Vol. 31, Issue 4 (2026), pp. 211–226 · Received: 9 October 2025 · Accepted: 17 February 2026*

## Abstract

**Background.** Time-restricted eating (TRE), which confines daily energy intake to a fixed window without prescribing calorie targets, has attracted interest as a simple dietary strategy for people with type 2 diabetes. Evidence from trials longer than 12 weeks remains limited.

**Methods.** In this single-centre, parallel-group randomised controlled trial, 155 adults aged 30–70 years with type 2 diabetes and HbA1c between 7.0% and 10.0% were assigned 1:1 to TRE (eating window 10:00–18:00 daily) or to usual dietary advice for 24 weeks. Both groups continued their glucose-lowering medication. The primary outcome was change in HbA1c from baseline to week 24. Secondary outcomes included body weight, fasting plasma glucose, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) metrics and adverse events.

**Results.** Of 155 randomised participants (78 TRE, 77 control), 141 (91%) completed the trial. Mean HbA1c decreased by 0.82 percentage points in the TRE group and by 0.29 points in the control group (between-group difference −0.53; 95% CI −0.78 to −0.28; *P* < 0.001). TRE also reduced body weight (difference −2.6 kg; 95% CI −3.7 to −1.5) and increased CGM time in range by 8.4 percentage points. Hypoglycaemic events were infrequent and similar between groups, although three participants in the TRE arm using sulfonylureas required dose reduction.

**Conclusions.** An 8-hour TRE schedule produced clinically meaningful improvements in glycaemic control over 24 weeks and was well tolerated. Medication review is advisable for patients using insulin secretagogues.

**Keywords:** type 2 diabetes; time-restricted eating; HbA1c; intermittent fasting; randomised controlled trial

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes affects a growing share of adults worldwide, and dietary modification remains a cornerstone of its management. Conventional advice focuses on what and how much to eat, typically through calorie restriction and changes to macronutrient composition. Adherence to such advice tends to decline after the first few months, partly because counting calories and portion sizes is demanding in daily life [1].

Time-restricted eating offers a different approach: it changes *when* people eat rather than *what* they eat. Participants are asked to consume all food within a daily window, usually between 6 and 10 hours, and to fast for the remaining hours while drinking water or other calorie-free beverages. Short-term studies in people with obesity or prediabetes have reported modest weight loss and improvements in insulin sensitivity, which have been attributed both to a spontaneous reduction in energy intake and to better alignment of food intake with circadian rhythms in glucose metabolism [2, 3].

For people with established type 2 diabetes, two questions are especially relevant. First, it is unclear whether the glycaemic benefits observed in short trials persist beyond three months. Second, prolonged daily fasting could raise the risk of hypoglycaemia among people treated with insulin or sulfonylureas. We therefore conducted a 24-week randomised controlled trial to assess the efficacy and safety of TRE as an addition to usual care.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Study design and participants

This was a single-centre, open-label, parallel-group randomised trial conducted at the outpatient diabetes clinic of Meridian University Hospital between March 2024 and August 2025. The protocol was approved by the institutional research ethics committee and all participants gave written informed consent[^1].

Eligible participants were aged 30–70 years, had a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes for at least 12 months, an HbA1c between 7.0% and 10.0% (53–86 mmol/mol), and a stable glucose-lowering regimen for at least three months. Key exclusion criteria were current insulin pump therapy, an estimated glomerular filtration rate below 45 mL/min/1.73 m², pregnancy or breastfeeding, a history of eating disorders, shift work involving night duty, and an existing habitual eating window shorter than 10 hours.

### 2.2 Randomisation and interventions

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio using computer-generated blocks of variable size (four or six), stratified by baseline HbA1c (< 8.5% vs ≥ 8.5%) and by use of insulin or sulfonylureas. Allocation was concealed through a central web-based system.

Participants in the TRE group were instructed to consume all foods and caloric beverages between 10:00 and 18:00 every day, with no restriction on the type or quantity of food. Water, black coffee, unsweetened tea and other non-caloric drinks were permitted outside the window. A dietitian provided a 30-minute introductory session and brief telephone follow-up at weeks 2, 6 and 12. Participants in the control group received standard dietary advice in line with national guidelines, delivered in a session of equal length, and were asked to maintain their usual meal timing.

At randomisation, the study physician reviewed medication for participants using insulin or sulfonylureas in the TRE group. Basal insulin doses were reduced by 10–20% when fasting glucose was below 7 mmol/L, and sulfonylurea doses could be halved if any glucose value below 3.9 mmol/L was recorded during follow-up.

### 2.3 Outcomes

The primary outcome was the change in HbA1c from baseline to week 24. Secondary outcomes were changes in body weight, waist circumference, fasting plasma glucose, fasting insulin, lipid profile and blood pressure, and CGM-derived metrics collected over 14 days at baseline and at week 24. Time in range was defined as the percentage of CGM readings between 3.9 and 10.0 mmol/L. Adherence was recorded by participants in a smartphone application that logged the timing of the first and last caloric intake each day; a day was considered adherent if all intake fell within the prescribed window with a tolerance of 30 minutes.

### 2.4 Statistical analysis

Assuming a standard deviation of 0.9 percentage points for the change in HbA1c, a sample of 64 participants per group provided 90% power to detect a between-group difference of 0.5 points at a two-sided significance level of 0.05. Allowing for 15% attrition, the target was 150 participants.

Analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle. The primary outcome was analysed with a linear mixed model including fixed effects for group, visit, the group-by-visit interaction and the stratification factors, with a random intercept for participant. Missing data were handled implicitly under a missing-at-random assumption; sensitivity analyses used multiple imputation and a per-protocol population (participants with ≥ 80% adherent days)[^2].

### 3.1 Participants

A total of 412 people were screened, of whom 155 were randomised: 78 to TRE and 77 to control. Fourteen participants (eight TRE, six control) did not attend the week-24 visit, mainly because of relocation or loss of interest. Median adherence in the TRE group was 86% of days (IQR 74–93%). The median daily eating window, as recorded in the application, decreased from 12.8 hours at baseline to 8.3 hours during the intervention. In the control group it remained essentially unchanged (12.5 hours).

### 3.2 Primary outcome

HbA1c decreased in both groups, with a substantially larger reduction in the TRE group (Table 2 and Figure 1). The adjusted between-group difference at week 24 was −0.53 percentage points (95% CI −0.78 to −0.28; *P* < 0.001). Most of the reduction occurred during the first 12 weeks; between weeks 12 and 24 HbA1c remained stable in the TRE group rather than rebounding. The multiple-imputation and per-protocol analyses gave consistent estimates of −0.51 and −0.61 points, respectively.

### 3.3 Secondary outcomes

Participants in the TRE group lost on average 3.5 kg compared with 0.9 kg in the control group. The difference in weight loss explained approximately 40% of the treatment effect on HbA1c in an exploratory mediation analysis, suggesting that the benefit of TRE was not solely a consequence of weight reduction. Fasting glucose, waist circumference and CGM time in range improved significantly with TRE. Changes in lipids, fasting insulin and blood pressure favoured TRE numerically but did not reach statistical significance.

Pre-specified subgroup analyses did not reveal heterogeneity of the treatment effect by sex, age group or baseline BMI. The effect on HbA1c appeared larger among participants with baseline HbA1c ≥ 8.5% (−0.71 points) than among those below this threshold (−0.42 points), although the interaction test was not significant (*P* = 0.09).

### 3.4 Safety

Adverse events are summarised in Table 3. The most common events attributed to the intervention were headache and hunger during the first two weeks, which resolved spontaneously in most cases. Level 1 hypoglycaemia (glucose 3.0–3.8 mmol/L) was reported by 9 participants in the TRE group and 7 in the control group. No level 3 (severe) hypoglycaemia occurred. Three TRE participants taking sulfonylureas had their dose halved after recurrent level 1 events; none of them discontinued the intervention.

## 4. Discussion

In this 24-week trial, an 8-hour time-restricted eating schedule added to usual care reduced HbA1c by about half a percentage point more than standard dietary advice. This effect size is comparable to that of adding a second-line oral glucose-lowering drug and is likely to be clinically relevant. Importantly, the improvement observed at week 12 was maintained at week 24, addressing a key uncertainty from earlier shorter trials.

Several mechanisms may contribute. Restricting the eating window likely reduced total energy intake, consistent with the observed weight loss. However, the mediation analysis suggests that weight change explains only part of the effect. Consuming food earlier in the day, when insulin sensitivity is generally higher, may improve postprandial glucose handling, and a longer overnight fast may reduce hepatic glucose output in the morning [4]. The 10:00–18:00 window chosen here was intended to balance these physiological considerations with feasibility for working adults; earlier windows might yield larger effects but could be harder to sustain[^3].

Safety is a central concern when recommending fasting regimens to people taking medication that can cause hypoglycaemia. In our trial, the frequency of hypoglycaemia was low and similar between groups, but this was achieved with proactive medication review. Clinicians who recommend TRE should therefore consider adjusting sulfonylurea and insulin doses at the outset and monitor glucose closely during the first weeks.

This study has limitations. It was conducted at a single centre and participants were not blinded to allocation, which may have influenced self-reported adherence. Dietary intake was not measured in detail, so we cannot separate the effects of meal timing from those of caloric reduction. Finally, the follow-up period, although longer than in most previous trials, does not allow conclusions about long-term outcomes such as cardiovascular events.

## 5. Conclusions

Time-restricted eating with a daily 8-hour window is an effective and well-tolerated strategy for improving glycaemic control in adults with type 2 diabetes over 24 weeks. It may be particularly attractive for patients who find calorie counting burdensome. Larger multicentre trials with longer follow-up are warranted.
