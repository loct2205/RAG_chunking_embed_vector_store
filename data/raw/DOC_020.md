## Abstract

Flaky tests – tests that both pass and fail on the same version of the code – erode developers' trust in continuous integration (CI), waste computing resources and delay releases. Most prior studies of flakiness rely on rerunning test suites in controlled environments, which may not reflect the conditions under which flakiness appears in practice. We analysed 36,134 CI runs from 120 active open-source projects written in seven programming languages, identifying 1,790 flaky tests among 318,329 tests (0.56%). We manually classified a stratified sample of 600 flaky tests into eight root-cause categories and examined how developers responded to them. Asynchronous waiting (26.5%), concurrency (15.5%) and test-order dependency (15.3%) were the most common causes. Projects written in JavaScript, TypeScript, Java and C# had higher median flaky rates than those in Python, Go and Rust. Only 41% of flaky tests in the sample were fixed within 90 days of first detection; 23% were disabled or annotated as skipped instead. We discuss implications for tool builders and for CI practices.

## 1. Introduction

Automated testing is central to modern software development. In continuous integration, every change pushed to a repository triggers a build and the execution of the test suite, and a failing test signals that the change has broken something. This signal is only useful if it is reliable. A flaky test produces different outcomes on repeated executions of the same code, typically because its result depends on factors that the test does not control, such as thread scheduling, network latency, the system clock or the order in which tests are executed [1].

The costs of flakiness are well documented in industrial reports. Developers spend time investigating failures unrelated to their changes, retry builds until they pass, and gradually learn to ignore red builds – a behaviour that allows real regressions to slip through. Large companies have reported that a significant share of test failures in their CI systems are due to flakiness rather than genuine bugs [2]. For open-source projects, which rely on volunteer contributors and limited CI budgets, the impact may be even more pronounced.

Research on flaky tests has grown rapidly, producing taxonomies of root causes, techniques for detecting flakiness by rerunning tests or analysing their code, and automated repair approaches. However, much of this work studies flakiness by rerunning tests many times in isolated environments. Flakiness observed in this way may differ from the flakiness that actually disrupts developers in CI, where tests run on shared cloud infrastructure, in parallel, and under varying load.

This paper reports an empirical study of flaky tests as they appear in real CI pipelines of open-source projects. We address three research questions:

- **RQ1 (Prevalence):** How common are flaky tests in open-source CI, and how does prevalence vary across projects and languages?
- **RQ2 (Root causes):** What are the root causes of flaky tests observed in CI?
- **RQ3 (Developer response):** How do developers respond to flaky tests, and how long do flaky tests persist?

## 2. Study Design

### 2.1 Project selection

We selected projects hosted on a public code-hosting platform that met the following criteria: (i) at least 500 stars; (ii) at least 100 commits in 2025; (iii) a CI configuration that runs the test suite on every pull request and on the main branch; and (iv) CI logs retained for at least six months and accessible through the platform's API. From 1,412 candidates we sampled 120 projects stratified by language. The final set comprises 36 Java, 25 Python, 20 JavaScript, 13 Go, 12 TypeScript, 8 C# and 6 Rust projects. Per-project statistics are listed in Appendix A[^1].

### 2.2 Identifying flaky tests

We collected CI runs between June and November 2025, excluding runs that failed before tests were executed (for example, because of compilation errors or dependency resolution failures). A test was labelled flaky if, for the same commit, it both passed and failed in different runs, including automatic or manual reruns. This conservative definition avoids misclassifying genuine regressions, at the cost of missing flaky tests that were never rerun on the same commit. To capture these, we additionally ran a "rerun experiment": for 200 randomly selected commits per project we reran the failing jobs up to five times on the same infrastructure.

For each test we computed a flakiness score, defined as the probability of failure across runs on commits where the test is known to be flaky:

$$
\mathrm{FS}(t) = \frac{\sum_{c \in C_t} f_{t,c}}{\sum_{c \in C_t} n_{t,c}} \tag{1}
$$

where $C_t$ is the set of commits on which test $t$ exhibited both outcomes, $f_{t,c}$ is the number of failing executions and $n_{t,c}$ the total number of executions of $t$ on commit $c$.

### 2.3 Root-cause classification

From the 1,790 flaky tests identified, we drew a sample of 600 stratified by project, with a maximum of 10 tests per project. Two authors independently inspected the test code, the failure logs, the code under test and, when available, the issue tracker and commit history, and assigned each test to one of eight root-cause categories adapted from prior taxonomies. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third author. Inter-rater agreement before discussion was substantial (Cohen's κ = 0.78).

## 3. Results

### 3.1 RQ1: Prevalence

Across the 120 projects, 1,790 of 318,329 tests (0.56%) were flaky during the six-month observation window. Although this fraction appears small, its impact is large: because a CI run fails if any test fails, a suite of several thousand tests with even a handful of flaky tests produces frequent spurious failures. In 49 projects (41%), at least ten tests were flaky. The median per-project flaky rate was 0.50%, and the highest rate, 2.95%, was observed in the JavaScript project *cobalt-queue*, where a large set of time-dependent UI tests failed around daylight-saving transitions.

Flaky rates differed by language. The median per-project rate was 0.58% for JavaScript and TypeScript, 0.56% for C# and 0.51% for Java, compared with 0.33% for Rust, 0.32% for Python and 0.30% for Go. We caution against interpreting these differences as properties of the languages themselves: they likely reflect the types of applications written in each language, such as browser-based front ends that rely heavily on asynchronous events, as well as differences in testing frameworks and conventions.

Figure 1 plots the flaky rate against the size of the test suite. Larger suites do not have higher flaky rates; if anything, the largest projects tend to have slightly lower rates, possibly because they invest more in test infrastructure and flakiness monitoring.

### 3.2 RQ2: Root causes

Table 1 and Figure 2 summarise the root causes in the sample of 600 flaky tests. Asynchronous waiting was the most frequent category (26.5%). These tests typically wait for an asynchronous operation – a network response, a UI rendering, a background job – using a fixed sleep or a timeout that is sufficient on a developer's machine but not under the variable load of shared CI runners. The following snippet, simplified from one of the studied projects, illustrates the pattern:

```python
def test_notification_is_sent(client, mailbox):
    client.post("/orders", json={"item": "book", "qty": 1})
    time.sleep(0.5)            # wait for background worker
    assert len(mailbox.messages) == 1
```

The fix adopted by the developers replaced the fixed sleep with polling until the condition holds or a generous timeout expires.

| Root cause | Description | n | % |
|:---|:---|---:|---:|
| Async wait | Fixed sleeps or timeouts for asynchronous operations | 159 | 26.5 |
| Concurrency | Data races, deadlocks, non-deterministic thread interleavings | 93 | 15.5 |
| Test order | Shared state between tests; dependence on execution order | 92 | 15.3 |
| Network | Calls to external services, DNS, port conflicts | 82 | 13.7 |
| Time | Dependence on wall-clock time, time zones, dates | 62 | 10.3 |
| Resource leak | Unreleased files, sockets, memory or database connections | 53 | 8.8 |
| Randomness | Unseeded random data or non-deterministic iteration order | 33 | 5.5 |

Concurrency and test-order dependency followed with similar frequencies. Test-order dependency was especially common in Java and Python projects that had recently enabled parallel or randomised test execution to speed up CI; enabling these features exposed hidden dependencies between tests that had previously always run in the same order. Network-related flakiness was most common in projects that integrate with third-party APIs in their test suites rather than using mocks or local test doubles.

We traced the history of each sampled flaky test for 90 days after its first detected flaky failure, looking for commits that modified the test or the code under test with the intent of addressing flakiness, as well as for annotations that disabled or skipped the test. Overall, 41.0% of flaky tests were fixed within 90 days. Fix rates were highest for test-order dependencies (50.0%) and asynchronous waits (46.6%), which are often addressable with localised changes to the test. Concurrency and network-related flakiness were fixed least often; instead, developers tended to disable these tests or add automatic retries. The median time to fix, among fixed tests, was 11 days. For the 128 tests with no action, the flaky failures continued throughout the observation window, and in several projects contributors explicitly noted in pull-request discussions that a known flaky test could be ignored[^2].

Retries deserve particular attention. While automatic retries reduce the number of red builds, they also hide genuine intermittent bugs in production code. In 9 of the 88 tests with retries added, we later found that the flakiness was caused by a real concurrency bug in the code under test that was subsequently reported by users.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Implications for developers

Our results suggest several practical recommendations. First, fixed sleeps in tests should be treated as a code smell; polling with explicit conditions and generous timeouts is almost always more robust. Second, projects that enable parallel or randomised test execution should expect to uncover hidden order dependencies and plan time to fix them, rather than reverting the change. Third, tests that call external network services should be isolated using local test doubles, with a small number of explicitly labelled integration tests run separately. Finally, retries should be used sparingly and tracked, because they can mask real bugs.

### 4.2 Implications for tool builders

Detection tools based on rerunning tests in isolation would miss a substantial fraction of the flakiness we observed, especially flakiness caused by resource contention on shared CI runners and by test-order dependencies that only appear with specific parallelisation settings. Tools that mine CI history across runs, as we did, can complement rerun-based detection at no additional computational cost. Automatic classification of root causes from failure logs also appears feasible for the most common categories: in a preliminary experiment, a simple classifier using log keywords correctly identified asynchronous-wait failures with an F1 score of 0.81.

### 4.3 Threats to validity

Our definition of flakiness requires the same commit to produce different outcomes, so flaky tests that were never rerun may be undercounted. The rerun experiment mitigates this but covers only a sample of commits. The manual classification involves subjective judgement, although inter-rater agreement was substantial. Finally, our sample of 120 open-source projects may not generalise to industrial codebases with different testing practices and infrastructure.

## 5. Related Work

Early empirical work established taxonomies of flaky-test root causes based on fixes in open-source projects [1]. Subsequent studies investigated flakiness in specific domains such as web front ends, machine learning code and mobile applications, and proposed detection techniques based on rerunning tests, analysing code coverage differences and machine-learning classifiers trained on test code [3]. Industrial studies have quantified the cost of flakiness and described infrastructure for quarantining flaky tests [2]. Our study complements this body of work by focusing on flakiness as observed in production CI pipelines and by tracking developers' responses over time.

## 6. Conclusion

Flaky tests are a persistent problem in open-source CI. Although only about half a percent of tests were flaky in our study, they affected four in ten projects substantially and were often left unfixed. Asynchronous waiting, concurrency and test-order dependency together account for more than half of all cases. We hope our findings and dataset help developers prioritise fixes and guide researchers towards detection and repair techniques that match the realities of CI.
