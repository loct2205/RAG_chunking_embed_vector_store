# Humidity-induced degradation pathways in mixed-cation perovskite solar cells and the protective role of a fluorinated interlayer

Jonas Lindqvist¹, Farah Al-Mansouri¹,²*, Chen Wei²

¹ Department of Materials Engineering, Kestrel University  
² Laboratory for Thin-Film Energy Devices, Eastvale College of Science  
\* Corresponding author: Farah Al-Mansouri (f.almansouri@kestrel-materials.example.org)

*Advanced Energy Materials Letters, Vol. 7, Article 2604411 (2026) · Received: 3 November 2025 · Accepted: 22 March 2026*

**Abstract:** Moisture remains the primary environmental stressor limiting the operational lifetime of metal halide perovskite solar cells. We combine in situ X-ray diffraction, photoluminescence mapping and device tracking to compare degradation pathways of formamidinium–caesium lead iodide cells with and without a thin fluorinated ammonium interlayer at the perovskite/hole-transport interface. At 85% relative humidity and 25 °C, unprotected devices lost 20% of their initial efficiency (T80) after 112 h, whereas devices with the interlayer reached T80 after 1,040 h. Degradation of unprotected films proceeds through formation of a hydrated intermediate followed by irreversible conversion to lead iodide, beginning at grain boundaries. The interlayer suppresses water ingress at grain boundaries and reduces the apparent activation energy of degradation from 0.62 to 0.41 eV, indicating a change in the rate-limiting step.

## 1. Introduction

Perovskite solar cells have reached certified power conversion efficiencies (PCE) comparable to those of crystalline silicon within little more than a decade of research. Their commercial deployment, however, depends on demonstrating lifetimes of 20–25 years under outdoor conditions. Among environmental stressors – heat, light, oxygen and moisture – moisture is particularly damaging because water molecules can coordinate with the organic cations and the lead halide framework, leading to decomposition of the perovskite phase [1].

Mixed-cation compositions such as formamidinium–caesium lead iodide (FA–Cs) show better intrinsic stability than early methylammonium-based perovskites, but they are still sensitive to humidity during operation and storage. Encapsulation can reduce water ingress, yet edge seals are never perfect and any water that penetrates will attack the most vulnerable regions of the film. Understanding where and how degradation begins is therefore essential for designing internal barrier layers that complement external encapsulation.

Here we investigate the humidity-induced degradation of FA₀.₈₅Cs₀.₁₅PbI₃ devices and evaluate a thin interlayer of a fluorinated ammonium salt deposited on the perovskite surface. Fluorinated alkyl chains are strongly hydrophobic, and ammonium head groups can passivate undercoordinated surface sites [2].

## 2. Experimental

**Device fabrication.** Devices were fabricated in an n–i–p architecture on fluorine-doped tin oxide glass with a tin oxide electron transport layer, a 550 nm perovskite absorber deposited by a two-step antisolvent method, a spiro-type hole transport layer and a gold electrode. For interlayer devices, a 1 mg/mL solution of the fluorinated ammonium iodide in isopropanol was spin-coated on the annealed perovskite film and annealed at 100 °C for 5 min. Table 1 lists the main characteristics of the two device types.

**Table 1.** Initial photovoltaic parameters (mean ± SD of 24 devices per group, reverse scan)

| Parameter | Control | Interlayer |
|:---|---:|---:|
| PCE (%) | 21.4 ± 0.5 | 22.1 ± 0.4 |
| Open-circuit voltage (V) | 1.112 ± 0.012 | 1.141 ± 0.010 |
| Short-circuit current (mA cm⁻²) | 24.8 ± 0.3 | 24.7 ± 0.3 |
| Fill factor (%) | 77.6 ± 1.1 | 78.4 ± 0.9 |
| Hysteresis index | 0.046 | 0.021 |
| Water contact angle (°) | 54 ± 3 | 101 ± 4 |

**Ageing protocol.** Unencapsulated devices were aged in a climate chamber at 25, 45 and 65 °C, each at 85% relative humidity (RH), in the dark at open circuit. Devices were removed periodically for current–voltage measurements under simulated AM1.5G illumination. Separate films on glass were monitored by in situ grazing-incidence X-ray diffraction and confocal photoluminescence mapping.

**Kinetic analysis.** The time to reach 80% of initial PCE, $T_{80}$, was extracted at each temperature. Assuming a thermally activated process, the apparent activation energy $E_a$ was obtained from

$$
\frac{1}{T_{80}} = A \exp\left(-\frac{E_a}{k_B T}\right) \tag{1}
$$

where $A$ is a pre-exponential factor, $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant and $T$ the absolute temperature.

## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1 Device stability

Figure 1 shows the evolution of normalised PCE at 25 °C and 85% RH. Control devices degraded steadily after an induction period of about 40 h and reached T80 after 112 h. Interlayer devices showed a slight initial increase in efficiency, which we attribute to continued surface passivation during the first hours of exposure, and retained 80% of their initial PCE for 1,040 h. The loss in control devices was dominated by fill factor and short-circuit current, consistent with the formation of insulating and non-absorbing phases.

### 3.2 Degradation pathway

In situ diffraction of control films revealed the emergence of a reflection at low angle after approximately 30 h, which we assign to a monohydrate phase. This hydrated intermediate grew for about 60 h, after which the characteristic lead iodide reflection appeared and grew while both the perovskite and hydrate signals decreased. Photoluminescence mapping showed that emission first dropped along grain boundaries, forming a network of dark lines, before spreading into grain interiors. These observations indicate that water enters primarily along grain boundaries, forms a hydrate that is initially reversible, and subsequently decomposes into lead iodide once the organic cation is lost.

For interlayer films, no hydrate reflection was detected for the first 400 h, and photoluminescence remained spatially uniform. Lead iodide formation began only after about 700 h and was spatially random rather than concentrated at grain boundaries. We interpret this as evidence that the interlayer seals the grain boundary openings at the film surface, so that degradation must proceed through slower diffusion across grains.

### 3.3 Temperature dependence

Table 2 summarises T80 values at three temperatures. Arrhenius fits yield apparent activation energies of 0.62 eV for control devices and 0.41 eV for interlayer devices. The lower activation energy, combined with much longer lifetimes, suggests a change in the rate-limiting step: in control devices degradation is limited by the chemical decomposition of the hydrate, whereas in interlayer devices it is limited by water transport through the hydrophobic layer, which is less temperature-sensitive.

**Table 2.** Time to 80% of initial PCE (T80, h) at 85% RH

| Temperature (°C) | Control | Interlayer | Improvement factor |
|:---|---:|---:|---:|
| 25 | 112 | 1,040 | 9.3 |
| 45 | 26 | 380 | 14.6 |
| 65 | 7.3 | 147 | 20.1 |
| Activation energy Eₐ (eV) | 0.62 | 0.41 | – |

The improvement factor increases with temperature, which is encouraging for applications in hot and humid climates. Extrapolating the Arrhenius relation should nevertheless be done with caution, because other degradation mechanisms such as ion migration may become dominant at high temperature or under illumination.

## 4. Conclusions

Humidity-induced degradation of FA–Cs perovskite solar cells starts at grain boundaries through a hydrated intermediate that converts irreversibly to lead iodide. A fluorinated ammonium interlayer improves the initial efficiency, raises the water contact angle by nearly 50°, and extends T80 at 25 °C and 85% RH from 112 h to 1,040 h. The interlayer changes the rate-limiting step of degradation and offers a simple, solution-processable complement to external encapsulation.
