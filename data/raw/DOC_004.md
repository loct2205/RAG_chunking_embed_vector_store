## Abstract

This paper examines whether international and domestic remittances help households in Southeast Asia smooth consumption when they experience adverse income shocks. Using a harmonised panel of 9,412 households from four countries observed over three survey waves between 2018 and 2024, we estimate the sensitivity of per capita consumption to idiosyncratic income changes and test whether this sensitivity is lower for households that receive remittances. We instrument income shocks with rainfall deviations and local labour-market closures. Households without remittance income reduce consumption by 0.31 percent for each one percent fall in income, compared with 0.17 percent for remittance-receiving households. The buffering effect is concentrated in food and education spending and is stronger when the migrant works abroad rather than in a domestic city. During the 2020–2021 pandemic period, however, the insurance value of domestic remittances largely disappeared because shocks hit migrants and origin households simultaneously.

## 1. Introduction

Households in low- and middle-income countries face large and frequent income fluctuations. Crop failures, illness, job loss and price shocks can reduce earnings sharply in a given year, while formal insurance and credit markets remain thin, especially in rural areas. A large literature has asked how well households manage to protect consumption from such shocks and which informal mechanisms they rely on, including savings in livestock or gold, borrowing from relatives, and risk-sharing within villages [1, 2].

Migration and the remittances that follow are an increasingly important part of this picture. When one or more household members work in another region or country, the household's income sources become less correlated with local conditions. In principle, a migrant can send additional money home when the origin household experiences a bad harvest or a health shock, providing a form of insurance that does not depend on local institutions[^1]. Whether remittances actually respond to shocks in this way is an empirical question, and the answer is likely to differ by the type of migration, the nature of the shock and the broader economic environment.

Southeast Asia is a particularly interesting setting. The region combines large flows of international labour migration, notably to East Asia and the Gulf states, with rapid internal migration from rural provinces to manufacturing hubs and capital cities. The COVID-19 pandemic offered an unusual natural experiment: for the first time in decades, shocks to destination labour markets were as severe as, and simultaneous with, shocks at origin.

We contribute in three ways. First, we construct a harmonised household panel covering four countries, which allows us to compare the role of remittances across institutional settings. Second, we address the endogeneity of income changes by using plausibly exogenous sources of variation. Third, we document how the insurance function of remittances changed during the pandemic, distinguishing between international and domestic migration.

## 2. Data

### 2.1 Survey sources and harmonisation

We combine data from national household surveys in four Southeast Asian countries, each with a panel component that re-interviews households across waves[^2]. The surveys share a common structure: detailed consumption modules covering food and non-food expenditure, income modules covering wages, self-employment and transfers, and household rosters that record the location of absent members. We harmonise consumption aggregates to a common definition, convert all monetary values to 2018 international dollars at purchasing power parity, and restrict the sample to households observed in all three waves.

The final sample contains 9,412 households and 28,236 household-year observations. Table 1 reports descriptive statistics. About 27 percent of households received remittances in at least one wave, with international remittances more common in the two countries with long-established overseas labour programmes.

### 2.2 Measuring income shocks

Changes in household income reflect both genuine shocks and anticipated changes, for example a planned move to a better job. To isolate the shock component, we use two instruments. The first is the deviation of growing-season rainfall in the household's district from its 30-year mean, which affects agricultural income. The second is an indicator for the closure or major lay-off at a large employer (more than 500 workers) within the household's district during the year preceding the survey, which affects wage income. Both instruments are plausibly unrelated to household-specific characteristics conditional on district and wave fixed effects.

## 3. Empirical strategy

Our baseline specification follows the standard consumption-insurance test. For household $i$ in wave $t$ we estimate

$$
\Delta \ln c_{it} = \beta\, \Delta \ln y_{it} + \gamma\, (\Delta \ln y_{it} \times R_{i}) + \delta R_{i} + \mathbf{x}_{it}^{\prime}\theta + \mu_{d} + \tau_{t} + \varepsilon_{it} \tag{1}
$$

where $c_{it}$ is per capita consumption, $y_{it}$ is per capita income excluding remittances, $R_i$ indicates that the household had a migrant member at baseline, $\mathbf{x}_{it}$ is a vector of demographic controls, and $\mu_d$ and $\tau_t$ are district and wave fixed effects. Under full insurance, $\beta$ would be zero. The coefficient of interest is $\gamma$: a negative value indicates that households with migrants are better insured.

Because $\Delta \ln y_{it}$ may be correlated with the error term, we estimate the equation by two-stage least squares, with the first stage

$$
\Delta \ln y_{it} = \pi_1 \mathrm{Rain}_{dt} + \pi_2 \mathrm{Closure}_{dt} + \mathbf{x}_{it}^{\prime}\lambda + \mu_d + \tau_t + u_{it} \tag{2}
$$

and analogous first stages for the interaction terms, using the instruments interacted with $R_i$. We also estimate how remittances themselves respond to shocks:

$$
\Delta r_{it} = \phi\, \Delta \ln y_{it} + \mathbf{x}_{it}^{\prime}\psi + \mu_d + \tau_t + e_{it} \tag{3}
$$

where $r_{it}$ is remittances received as a share of baseline income. A negative $\phi$ indicates that remittances increase when income falls[^3]. Standard errors are clustered at the district level throughout.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Consumption sensitivity to income shocks

Table 2 reports the main estimates. Ordinary least squares (column 1) suggests a modest elasticity of consumption to income of 0.19, which is likely attenuated by measurement error in income. The instrumental variable estimates in columns 2 to 4 are larger. In our preferred specification (column 3), households without migrants reduce consumption by 0.31 percent for every one percent fall in income. The interaction term is −0.14 and statistically significant, implying an elasticity of about 0.17 for migrant households, roughly 45 percent lower. Column 4 distinguishes international from domestic migration and shows that the buffering effect is driven mostly by international migrants.

### 4.2 Do remittances respond to shocks?

The buffering effect could arise because remittances increase after shocks or simply because migrant households have higher and more stable income overall. Estimates of the remittance response equation support the first channel. For households with international migrants, a ten percent fall in income is associated with an increase in remittances equal to 1.6 percent of baseline income ($\hat{\phi} = -0.16$, standard error 0.05). For households with domestic migrants, the response is smaller and imprecisely estimated ($\hat{\phi} = -0.06$, standard error 0.04).

### 4.3 Which spending is protected?

Table 3 decomposes the consumption response by expenditure category. Remittances protect food consumption and education spending most strongly. In non-migrant households, a one percent fall in income reduces education spending by 0.52 percent, consistent with evidence that schooling is often the first item cut in hard times. In migrant households this elasticity falls to 0.21. Spending on durables and festivals responds strongly in both groups, which is consistent with households postponing discretionary purchases.

### 4.4 The pandemic period

The third survey wave covers the years 2020–2021 for two of the four countries. Re-estimating the main specification for this period produces a striking pattern. The interaction for international migrants remains negative but is smaller (−0.12), while the interaction for domestic migrants becomes positive (+0.05) and insignificant. Domestic migrants working in factories, construction and hospitality in large cities were themselves hit by lockdowns and lay-offs, and many returned home, adding to the number of dependants rather than sending money[^4]. In effect, the correlation between income at origin and destination, which underpins the insurance value of migration, rose sharply.

## 5. Robustness

We conducted several robustness checks. Using only the rainfall instrument, which is available for rural households, gives a slightly larger baseline elasticity (0.34) and a similar interaction (−0.15). Dropping one country at a time changes the interaction coefficient by no more than 0.03. Defining migrant status using the presence of any absent member, rather than members who sent money at baseline, produces nearly identical results. Finally, we address concerns that the timing of migration responds to shocks by restricting the sample to households whose migration status did not change across waves[^5].

## 6. Conclusion

Remittances play a meaningful role in helping Southeast Asian households protect consumption from income shocks, reducing the pass-through of income losses to consumption by close to half. The insurance is strongest for households with international migrants and for essential spending on food and education. The pandemic experience illustrates an important limitation: when shocks are systemic, informal insurance through domestic migration can fail precisely when it is needed most. Policies that lower the cost of sending money across borders and that extend social protection to returning migrants could strengthen the resilience of migrant-sending communities.
