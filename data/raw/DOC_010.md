# What drives ridership on bus rapid transit corridors? A short communication on station-level determinants

Isabel Fonseca¹*, Kwame Asante¹

¹ Urban Mobility Laboratory, Brightwater Polytechnic  
\* Corresponding author: Isabel Fonseca (i.fonseca@urbanmobility-lab.example.org)

*Transport Policy Notes, Short Communication, Vol. 12 (2026), 100341 · Received: 2 February 2026 · Accepted: 30 April 2026*

## Highlights

- Station-level boardings on three BRT corridors vary more than tenfold.
- Population and jobs within 800 m explain about half of the variation.
- Feeder bus connections and safe pedestrian crossings matter as much as density.
- Park-and-ride capacity has little effect on weekday boardings.

## Abstract

Bus rapid transit (BRT) has been adopted by many cities as a lower-cost alternative to rail, yet ridership often falls short of forecasts. This short communication examines what explains differences in boardings between stations, using automated fare collection data from 87 stations on three corridors in a mid-sized metropolitan area. Activity density near stations is the strongest single predictor, but the number of connecting feeder routes and the presence of signalised pedestrian crossings each have effects of a similar magnitude. The results suggest that BRT planning should treat station access as an integral part of the system rather than a secondary design detail.

## 1. Introduction

Since the late 1990s, bus rapid transit has spread from a handful of pioneering cities to more than 150 urban areas worldwide. Its appeal is straightforward: dedicated lanes, level boarding, off-board fare collection and high-frequency service can deliver travel times close to those of light rail at a fraction of the capital cost. Nevertheless, evaluations frequently report that ridership grows more slowly than projected, and that a few stations account for a large share of all boardings while others are lightly used.

Corridor-level studies tend to emphasise network coverage and service frequency. Far less attention has been paid to differences between stations on the same corridor, which share the same frequency and travel speed and therefore offer a cleaner view of how the surrounding environment shapes demand. This note reports a station-level analysis designed to inform planners who are deciding where to place stations and how to prioritise investment in access.

## 2. Data and approach

We used smart card tap-in records for all weekday trips during October 2025 on three BRT corridors with a combined length of 46 km and 87 stations. Average weekday boardings ranged from 310 at the least used station to 4,120 at a downtown interchange. For each station we compiled residential population and employment within an 800 m walking catchment, the number of feeder bus routes stopping within 150 m, the presence of a signalised pedestrian crossing providing direct access to the median platform, park-and-ride capacity, and distance to the city centre.

We estimated a log-linear regression of average weekday boardings on these variables, with corridor fixed effects to absorb differences in route length and service patterns. Coefficients are interpreted as approximate percentage changes in boardings.

## 3. Findings

Activity density was the strongest predictor. A 10% increase in combined population and jobs within the catchment was associated with a 5.2% increase in boardings, and density alone explained about 48% of the variation between stations. This confirms the conventional wisdom that BRT performs best where it serves dense, mixed-use areas.

Access variables, however, were also important. Each additional feeder route was associated with 7.9% more boardings, and stations with a signalised crossing directly to the platform recorded 31% more boardings than otherwise similar stations where passengers had to use footbridges or unsignalised crossings. Interviews conducted at six stations suggested that footbridges are a deterrent for older passengers, people carrying goods, and parents with small children.

Park-and-ride capacity had a small and statistically insignificant association with weekday boardings. Only four suburban stations had parking facilities, and occupancy surveys showed that they were on average less than half full. Distance to the centre lost its significance once density was controlled for, suggesting that peripheral stations are not inherently unattractive but are typically located in less dense areas.

Together, the model explained 71% of the variance in station-level boardings. The largest positive residuals occurred at stations near universities and hospitals, which generate demand throughout the day but are not fully captured by residential and employment counts.

## 4. Implications

Three practical implications follow. First, station placement should continue to prioritise dense activity centres, and land-use policy should encourage development within walking distance of existing stations. Second, investment in safe, at-grade pedestrian access and in feeder bus integration may yield ridership gains comparable to densification, at lower cost and on a shorter timescale. Third, park-and-ride facilities on urban BRT corridors appear to offer limited benefits and could be reconsidered in favour of land uses that generate walk-in demand.

The analysis is cross-sectional and based on a single metropolitan area, so it cannot establish causality, and the effects of access improvements should ideally be tested through before-and-after studies when new crossings or feeder routes are introduced.
