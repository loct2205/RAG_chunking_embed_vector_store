## Abstract

*Microservice applications fan out each user request to tens of backend services, so the end-to-end latency is dominated by the slowest call. Request hedging, which sends a duplicate request after a fixed delay, is a popular remedy, but under bursty load it can amplify congestion exactly when the system is most fragile. We present LATCH, a load-aware hedging policy that adapts the hedging delay to real-time queue estimates and suppresses hedges when replicas are saturated. We implement LATCH as a sidecar proxy and evaluate it on three open-source benchmark applications with production-derived traffic traces. Compared with fixed-delay hedging, LATCH reduces 99th-percentile latency by 31–46% at moderate load and avoids the latency collapse that fixed hedging exhibits above 80% utilisation, while sending 58% fewer duplicate requests.*

**Keywords:** tail latency, microservices, request hedging, load balancing, bursty traffic

## 1. Introduction

Interactive cloud services increasingly follow a microservice architecture in which a single user-facing request triggers a tree of internal remote procedure calls. A social media timeline request, for example, may touch authentication, user profile, graph, ranking, media and advertising services, each of which may itself call storage and caching layers. Because the parent request must wait for its slowest child, the probability that at least one call experiences a slow response grows rapidly with fan-out. If each of 50 independent calls has a 1% chance of exceeding 100 ms, roughly 40% of user requests will be slower than 100 ms. This "tail at scale" effect makes the 99th and 99.9th percentile latencies of individual services the dominant concern for operators [1].

Sources of latency variability are many: garbage collection pauses, CPU throttling in containers, noisy neighbours, network retransmissions and transient queueing. Request hedging addresses all of them without needing to identify the cause. The client sends a request to one replica and, if no response arrives within a hedging delay $d$, sends a second copy to another replica, using whichever response arrives first. Setting $d$ to the 95th percentile of observed latency bounds the extra load at about 5% in steady state.

The weakness of fixed-delay hedging appears under bursty load. When a traffic spike raises queueing delays at all replicas, many requests exceed $d$ simultaneously, triggering a wave of hedges that further increases queue lengths. In our measurements, a 2× burst lasting 10 seconds caused the hedge rate of a fixed policy to jump from 5% to 37%, and 99th-percentile latency to rise eightfold. This positive feedback loop resembles retry storms well known from distributed systems practice [2].

This paper makes the following contributions:

- We characterise the interaction between hedging and bursty load in three microservice benchmarks and show when hedging harms tail latency.
- We design LATCH, which sets the hedging delay from per-replica queue estimates and applies a hedge budget that shrinks as utilisation rises.
- We implement LATCH in a sidecar proxy with no application changes and evaluate it with production-derived traces.

## 2. Background and Motivation

We model each replica as a single queue with service time distribution $S$ and arrival rate $\lambda$. For an M/G/1 queue, the mean waiting time is given by the Pollaczek–Khinchine formula:

$$
W_q = \frac{\lambda\, \mathbb{E}[S^2]}{2\,(1-\rho)} \tag{1}
$$

where $\rho = \lambda\,\mathbb{E}[S]$ is utilisation. A hedging policy that duplicates a fraction $h$ of requests effectively raises the arrival rate to $\lambda(1+h)$, so the waiting time grows as $1/(1-\rho(1+h))$. At $\rho = 0.5$, a hedge rate of 10% increases mean waiting time by 11%; at $\rho = 0.85$ the same hedge rate increases it by 140%. Hedging is therefore cheap at low utilisation but dangerous near saturation, which is precisely when tail latency is worst and hedging seems most attractive.

## 3. Design of LATCH

### 3.1 Queue-aware hedging delay

Instead of a fixed percentile, LATCH computes the hedging delay per request from the expected completion time at the primary replica and at the best alternative. Each sidecar maintains an exponentially weighted moving average of in-flight requests $q_r$ and service time $\bar{s}_r$ for every replica $r$, using piggybacked load reports in response headers. The expected remaining time of a request sent to replica $p$ at time $t_0$ is estimated as $\hat{T}_p = (q_p + 1)\,\bar{s}_p - (t - t_0)$. A hedge to replica $a$ is worthwhile only if the alternative is expected to finish earlier by a margin $m$:

$$
\mathrm{hedge}(t) = \mathbb{1}\left[ (q_a + 1)\,\bar{s}_a + m < \hat{T}_p(t) \right] \tag{2}
$$

### 3.2 Adaptive hedge budget

Even well-targeted hedges add load. LATCH therefore enforces a token-bucket budget whose refill rate $b$ decreases with the cluster-wide utilisation estimate $\hat{\rho}$:

$$
b(\hat{\rho}) = b_{\max} \cdot \max\left(0,\ 1 - \left(\frac{\hat{\rho}}{\rho_c}\right)^{k}\right) \tag{3}
$$

We use $b_{\max} = 0.10$, $\rho_c = 0.9$ and $k = 4$, which allows close to the full budget below 60% utilisation and suppresses hedging entirely at 90%. The complete decision procedure is shown below.

```
on_timer(req):
    p  <- req.primary
    a  <- argmin_{r != p} (q[r] + 1) * s[r]
    if not bucket.try_take():      # budget exhausted
        return
    if (q[a] + 1) * s[a] + m < remaining(p, req):
        send_copy(req, a)
        req.hedged <- true
on_response(req, r):
    if first response: deliver; cancel(req, other)
    update_ewma(q[r], s[r], header.load)
```

### 3.3 Cancellation

When the first response arrives, LATCH sends a best-effort cancellation to the other replica. Cancellation recovers on average 62% of the duplicate work in our benchmarks, because many hedged requests are still waiting in the queue when cancelled.

## 4. Implementation

LATCH is implemented as a filter of approximately 2,400 lines in an open-source sidecar proxy. Load reports are carried in a four-byte response header containing the replica's queue length and a quantised service time. The filter uses a timer wheel with 1 ms resolution to schedule hedge checks. Configuration requires only the margin $m$ and budget parameters; the remaining state is learned online.

### 5.1 Setup

We evaluate on a 24-node cluster, each node with 32 cores and 128 GB memory, connected by 25 Gb/s Ethernet. Table 1 summarises the three benchmark applications. Load is generated by an open-loop client that replays arrival processes derived from anonymised production traces, scaled to target average utilisations between 40% and 90%. Bursts are injected by multiplying the arrival rate by 1.5–3× for 5–30 seconds.

We compare four policies: no hedging (None); fixed delay at the 95th percentile (Fixed-p95); a tied-request policy that enqueues copies at two replicas and cancels the slower one (Tied); and LATCH.

### 5.2 Tail latency

Table 2 reports 99th-percentile end-to-end latency under the bursty trace at three utilisation levels. At 60% utilisation, all hedging policies improve over None, with LATCH achieving the lowest latency on all applications. At 80% utilisation, Fixed-p95 becomes worse than no hedging on SocialFeed and MediaStream, while LATCH continues to improve tail latency by 31–46% relative to Fixed-p95. At 90%, LATCH effectively disables hedging and its latency converges to that of None, whereas Fixed-p95 and Tied suffer a latency collapse.

### 5.3 Overhead and ablation

LATCH sends 58% fewer duplicate requests than Fixed-p95 averaged across all experiments. The CPU overhead of the filter is below 1.5% of a core per thousand requests per second. Table 3 isolates the contribution of each component on SocialFeed at 80% utilisation.

**Table 3.** Ablation on SocialFeed at 80% utilisation

| Variant | p99 (ms) | Hedge rate (%) |
|:---|---:|---:|
| LATCH (full) | 191 | 3.1 |
| without hedge budget | 263 | 9.8 |
| without queue-aware delay | 241 | 4.7 |
| without cancellation | 214 | 3.1 |
| Fixed-p95 | 352 | 14.6 |

Removing the adaptive budget has the largest impact, raising p99 latency by 38%. The queue-aware delay contributes mostly by directing hedges to genuinely less loaded replicas; without it, a hedge often lands on a replica that is just as congested.

## 6. Related Work

Hedged and tied requests were popularised by large-scale web search systems [1]. Subsequent work has proposed replica selection based on feedback from servers [3] and admission control to prevent retry storms [2]. Closest to our work, adaptive hedging policies based on latency percentiles have been explored for storage systems [4], but they do not account for the load that hedges themselves add. LATCH combines replica load feedback with a utilisation-dependent budget.

## 7. Conclusion

Request hedging is an effective tool against tail latency, but naive policies can turn a traffic burst into a latency collapse. LATCH makes hedging load-aware by estimating queueing at each replica and by shrinking the hedge budget as utilisation grows. Our evaluation shows that this simple change preserves the benefits of hedging at moderate load and removes its risks near saturation.
