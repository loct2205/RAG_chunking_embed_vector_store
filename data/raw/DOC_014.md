# Habitual sleep duration and working memory performance in university students: a cross-sectional study with actigraphy

Charlotte Ainsworth¹, Mateo Ruiz-Delgado¹, Hana Novotná²*

¹ Department of Psychology, Eastvale University  
² Sleep and Cognition Unit, Halden University  
\* Corresponding author: Hana Novotná (h.novotna@halden-psych.example.org)

*Journal of Student Health and Cognition, Vol. 9, No. 3 (2026), pp. 141–152 · Received: 18 November 2025 · Accepted: 11 March 2026*

## Abstract

Insufficient sleep is common among university students and may impair cognitive functions that are central to learning. We examined the association between habitual sleep duration, measured objectively with wrist actigraphy over seven nights, and working memory performance in 184 undergraduate students. Working memory was assessed with a 2-back and 3-back task on the eighth day. Mean actigraphic sleep duration was 6.6 hours per night, and 43% of participants slept less than 6.5 hours on average. Shorter sleep was associated with lower accuracy on the 3-back task (r = 0.31, p < .001) but not on the 2-back task. Participants who slept less than 6.5 hours had a mean 3-back accuracy 7.8 percentage points lower than those who slept 7.5 hours or more, after adjusting for age, sex, caffeine use and chronotype. Night-to-night variability in sleep duration was independently associated with poorer performance. The findings suggest that both the amount and regularity of sleep matter for demanding working memory tasks.

## Introduction

Sleep plays an essential role in the maintenance of attention, executive functions and memory. Laboratory studies of sleep deprivation consistently show that even a single night of restricted sleep reduces performance on tasks requiring sustained attention and the manipulation of information in working memory [1]. Working memory – the capacity to hold and update information over short periods – underpins reasoning, reading comprehension and mathematical problem solving, making it a key determinant of academic achievement.

University students are a population at particular risk of insufficient sleep. Academic deadlines, part-time work, social activities and evening use of electronic devices often delay bedtime, while early classes constrain wake-up times. Surveys typically report that between one third and one half of students sleep less than seven hours on school nights [2]. Most of this evidence, however, relies on self-reported sleep, which tends to overestimate actual sleep duration. In addition, few studies have examined the regularity of sleep, even though irregular schedules may disrupt circadian rhythms independently of total sleep time.

The present study used actigraphy to measure sleep objectively over one week and tested two hypotheses: first, that shorter habitual sleep is associated with poorer working memory performance, especially at high cognitive load; and second, that greater night-to-night variability in sleep duration is associated with poorer performance after accounting for average sleep duration.

## Method

### Participants

Undergraduate students aged 18–25 years were recruited through course announcements and posters. Exclusion criteria were a diagnosed sleep disorder, current use of medication affecting sleep or cognition, shift work, and travel across more than two time zones in the preceding month. Of 203 students enrolled, 19 were excluded because of insufficient actigraphy data (fewer than five valid nights), leaving 184 participants (121 women, 63 men; mean age 20.4 years, SD = 1.7). Participants received course credit or a small payment.

### Procedure and measures

Participants wore a wrist actigraph on the non-dominant arm for seven consecutive days and nights during a regular teaching period, avoiding examination weeks. Sleep onset and offset were scored with a validated algorithm and checked against a sleep diary. For each participant we calculated mean sleep duration and the standard deviation of sleep duration across nights. On the eighth day, between 10:00 and 13:00, participants completed computerised 2-back and 3-back tasks with letters as stimuli, each comprising 90 trials. Accuracy was defined as the proportion of correct responses. Participants also completed questionnaires on caffeine consumption and chronotype.

## Results

Descriptive statistics and correlations are presented in Table 1. Mean sleep duration was 6.62 hours (SD = 0.71), and the mean night-to-night standard deviation was 1.08 hours. Sleep duration correlated positively with 3-back accuracy (r = .31, p < .001) but only weakly and non-significantly with 2-back accuracy (r = .12, p = .10). Sleep variability correlated negatively with 3-back accuracy (r = −.24, p = .001).

In a multiple regression predicting 3-back accuracy, mean sleep duration (β = .26, p < .001) and sleep variability (β = −.16, p = .03) were both significant predictors after controlling for age, sex, caffeine consumption and chronotype. The model explained 15% of the variance. When participants were grouped by mean sleep duration, those sleeping less than 6.5 hours (n = 79) had an adjusted mean 3-back accuracy of 67.9%, compared with 72.4% for those sleeping 6.5–7.5 hours (n = 76) and 75.7% for those sleeping 7.5 hours or more (n = 29). ## Discussion

Objectively measured short sleep was associated with poorer working memory performance under high cognitive load, while performance on the easier 2-back task was largely unaffected. This pattern is consistent with the view that sleep loss first impairs the most demanding executive processes, whereas simpler tasks can be maintained through compensatory effort. The independent association of sleep variability with performance suggests that irregular sleep schedules carry an additional cost, possibly through circadian misalignment.

The cross-sectional design does not allow causal conclusions; students with lower working memory capacity might also have less organised schedules. Nevertheless, the results support interventions that encourage not only longer but also more regular sleep, such as avoiding very early classes and promoting consistent bedtimes during the semester.
