# Non-stationary flood frequency analysis for a large monsoon river basin: implications for design floods under a changing climate

Nguyen Hoai Nam¹, Sarah E. Whitcombe²*, Rui Tavares²

¹ Centre for Water Resources Engineering, Eastvale College of Science  
² Centre for Applied Hydrology, Kestrel University  
\* Corresponding author: Sarah E. Whitcombe (s.whitcombe@kestrel-hydro.example.org)

*Journal of Hydrological Risk, Vol. 58 (2026), 104219 · Received: 12 August 2025 · Accepted: 5 March 2026*

## Abstract

Design floods for dams, levees and bridges are traditionally estimated under the assumption that annual maximum discharges are independent and identically distributed. This assumption is increasingly questioned in basins affected by climate change and land-use change. We fit stationary and non-stationary generalised extreme value (GEV) models to 65 years (1961–2025) of annual maximum discharge at the main gauging station of the Bach Long River, a monsoon-dominated basin of about 52,000 km², and transfer the results to seven other stations. A model with a linearly increasing location parameter is preferred by information criteria and likelihood-ratio tests, with a trend of 14.8 m³/s per year (about 1.6% per decade). Under this model, the effective 100-year flood for 2025 is 16,530 m³/s, 3.0% higher than the stationary estimate, and is projected to reach 16,900 m³/s by 2050. The record flood of 2024 (17,210 m³/s) corresponds to a return period of roughly 150 years under the stationary model but about 120 years under the non-stationary model evaluated for 2024. We discuss how non-stationary estimates can be incorporated into design practice through the concept of design life levels.

**Highlights:** (i) Significant upward trend in annual maximum floods over 1961–2025; (ii) non-stationary GEV model preferred over stationary alternatives; (iii) design floods should be evaluated over the project lifetime rather than for a single reference year.

## 1. Introduction

Flood frequency analysis (FFA) provides the statistical basis for designing hydraulic infrastructure and for flood risk mapping. In its classical form, a probability distribution is fitted to a series of annual maximum discharges and used to estimate the discharge with a given annual exceedance probability, for example the 100-year flood with a 1% chance of being exceeded in any year. The approach assumes that the statistical properties of floods do not change over time, an assumption often summarised as "stationarity is dead" in the hydrological literature of the last two decades [1].

Evidence for changes in flood regimes comes from several directions. Warming increases the water-holding capacity of the atmosphere by roughly 7% per degree Celsius, which may intensify heavy rainfall. Deforestation, urbanisation and river channel modification alter runoff generation and flood routing. Reservoir construction can reduce flood peaks downstream but also changes the shape of the flood frequency curve. In monsoon basins of Southeast Asia, interannual variability linked to the El Niño–Southern Oscillation adds further complexity [2].

Non-stationary FFA addresses these issues by allowing the parameters of the flood distribution to vary with time or with physically meaningful covariates. Although the methods are well established, their adoption in engineering practice has been slow, partly because the concept of a "return period" becomes ambiguous when probabilities change from year to year. This paper has three objectives: (1) to test for non-stationarity in the long annual maximum series of the Bach Long River; (2) to compare stationary and non-stationary GEV models; and (3) to illustrate the consequences for design flood estimates across the basin.

## 2. Study area and data

The Bach Long River drains a basin of approximately 52,000 km² with a tropical monsoon climate. About 75% of annual rainfall occurs between June and October, and annual maximum floods typically result from multi-day rainfall associated with tropical depressions and typhoons. The basin is predominantly mountainous in its upper part and contains extensive agricultural floodplains downstream. Two medium-sized reservoirs were commissioned in 1989 and 2011 on tributaries, together controlling about 9% of the drainage area at the main station[^1].

We use daily discharge records from eight gauging stations (Table 1). The main analysis focuses on station BL-03 (Bach Long), which has the longest and most complete record from 1961 to 2025. The full annual maximum series is listed in Appendix A. The mean annual maximum discharge is 9,026 m³/s, ranging from 5,420 m³/s in 1969 to 17,210 m³/s in 2024. The five largest floods occurred in 2024, 1971, 2008, 1996 and 1998.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Trend detection

Monotonic trends in the annual maximum series were tested with the Mann–Kendall test, using a variance correction for serial correlation. The magnitude of trend was estimated with the Theil–Sen slope estimator. We also applied the Pettitt test to detect possible abrupt change points, for instance related to reservoir commissioning.

### 3.2 Stationary GEV model

The GEV distribution has cumulative distribution function

$$
F(x;\mu,\sigma,\xi) = \exp\left\{ -\left[ 1 + \xi \left( \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} \right) \right]^{-1/\xi} \right\} \tag{1}
$$

defined for $1 + \xi (x-\mu)/\sigma > 0$, where $\mu$ is the location, $\sigma > 0$ the scale and $\xi$ the shape parameter. Positive values of $\xi$ indicate a heavy upper tail. The return level $x_T$ associated with return period $T$ years is obtained by inverting the distribution function:

$$
x_T = \mu + \frac{\sigma}{\xi} \left[ \left( -\ln\left(1 - \frac{1}{T}\right) \right)^{-\xi} - 1 \right] \tag{2}
$$

### 3.3 Non-stationary models

In the non-stationary models, the location and/or scale parameters vary linearly with time $t$ (years since 1961):

$$
\mu(t) = \mu_0 + \mu_1 t, \qquad \ln \sigma(t) = \sigma_0 + \sigma_1 t \tag{3}
$$

The logarithmic link for the scale parameter ensures positivity. The shape parameter is kept constant because it is difficult to estimate reliably even from long records. We consider four models: stationary (M0), linear trend in location (M1), linear trend in scale (M2), and trends in both (M3). Parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood, maximising

$$
\ell(\theta) = -\sum_{t=1}^{n} \left\{ \ln \sigma(t) + \left(1 + \frac{1}{\xi}\right) \ln\left[1 + \xi z_t\right] + \left[1 + \xi z_t\right]^{-1/\xi} \right\} \tag{4}
$$

with $z_t = (x_t - \mu(t))/\sigma(t)$. Models are compared using the Akaike information criterion $\mathrm{AIC} = 2k - 2\ell$, the Bayesian information criterion and likelihood-ratio tests for nested models. Confidence intervals for return levels are obtained by the profile likelihood method.

### 3.4 Effective return levels and design life levels

Under non-stationarity, the $T$-year return level for a specific year $t$ (the "effective return level") is obtained by substituting $\mu(t)$ and $\sigma(t)$ into the return level equation. For design purposes, we also compute the design life level: the discharge that has a probability $p$ of being exceeded at least once during a project lifetime spanning years $t_1$ to $t_2$[^2].

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Trend analysis

The Mann–Kendall test indicates a significant increasing trend in annual maximum discharge at BL-03 (Kendall's τ = 0.21, *p* = 0.013). The Theil–Sen slope is 15.6 m³/s per year, close to the maximum likelihood estimate from the non-stationary model presented below. The mean of annual maxima over the most recent 30 years (9,656 m³/s) exceeds that of the first 30 years (8,582 m³/s) by 12.5%. The Pettitt test did not detect a significant change point (*p* = 0.18), suggesting a gradual change rather than a step associated with reservoir construction. Figure 1 shows the series together with the fitted median of the non-stationary model.

### 4.3 Return levels

Figure 2 compares return level curves for the stationary model and for the non-stationary model evaluated in 2025 and 2050. The 100-year flood at BL-03 is 16,050 m³/s under the stationary model, 16,530 m³/s for 2025 under M1, and 16,900 m³/s for 2050 if the trend continues. The differences are larger than they might appear: under the stationary model, the effective 2050 100-year flood would correspond to a return period of about 125 years, whereas under M1 the stationary 100-year flood corresponds to an exceedance probability of 1.3% in 2025 and 1.6% in 2050.

### 4.4 Design life levels

For a structure with a design life from 2026 to 2075 and an acceptable probability of 39.5% of at least one exceedance during that period, which corresponds to the conventional 100-year standard for a 50-year life under stationarity, the design life level under M1 is 16,870 m³/s. This is 5.1% higher than the stationary 100-year flood. For a levee upgrade with a 30-year design life, the corresponding increase is 3.6%.

## 5. Discussion

The results provide consistent evidence that annual maximum floods on the Bach Long River have increased over the last six decades. The absence of an abrupt change point and the presence of trends at most stations suggest a basin-wide driver rather than local effects of reservoir operation. Plausible contributing factors include the intensification of extreme rainfall associated with tropical cyclones, conversion of forest to agriculture in the upper basin, and channel narrowing caused by floodplain encroachment. Disentangling these factors would require covariate-based models using, for example, basin-averaged extreme rainfall and land-cover indices, which we plan to develop in future work.

A common objection to non-stationary FFA is that extrapolating a linear trend far into the future is speculative. We share this concern and therefore recommend that non-stationary estimates be used as a sensitivity analysis alongside stationary estimates rather than replacing them, and that projections beyond 2050 be avoided unless supported by physically based climate and land-use scenarios. Uncertainty is also substantial: the 95% confidence interval of the trend parameter spans from about 4 to 25 m³/s per year, and confidence intervals for 100-year return levels are roughly ±15%.

From a practical perspective, the design life level offers a transparent way to incorporate non-stationarity into engineering standards. It retains the familiar notion of an acceptable risk over the life of a project while accounting for changing probabilities. Adopting this concept would require revisions to national design guidelines, which currently specify design floods only in terms of return periods[^3].

## 6. Conclusions

Using 65 years of data, we found a statistically significant upward trend in annual maximum floods of the Bach Long River. A GEV model with a linearly increasing location parameter is clearly preferred to the stationary model. Design floods estimated under stationarity may underestimate current and future flood hazard by 3–5% for the 100-year standard, with larger differences for longer design lives. We recommend that flood frequency studies in the basin routinely report non-stationary estimates and design life levels alongside conventional results.
